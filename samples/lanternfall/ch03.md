Five winters later the new sea wall stood a full fathom higher than the old one, and the lower town had been redrawn so many times that children learned the streets from Mara's maps before they learned them with their feet.

The thirty-six-year-old Mara Venn kept her office in the old harbour building now. Her hair, cropped short since the year of the fever, had gone the red of rust. Visitors who had known her before the flood sometimes failed to recognise her until she looked up from the drafting table. Mara's eyes were green, and they had not changed: they still counted everything.

Tomas came on the first of every month with the harbour dispatches. Tomas, now thirty-one, had given up swimming for a horse and given up the horse for a desk, and complained about both.

"The Hollin road," he said, dropping a letter on her table. "They want it resurveyed."

"It's still wrong?"

"It's still in the marsh."

* * *

The light still turned above the harbour. A younger keeper tended it now; the Warden had taken a cottage on the cliff road and sent Mara a jar of plum preserves every autumn, with no note, which was how Ilsa said everything.

Mara spread the old survey beside the new one and began, again, to count.
