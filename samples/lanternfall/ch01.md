The sea wall at Saltmere broke an hour after midnight, and by dawn the lower town was a map nobody had drawn yet.

Mara Venn stood on the lighthouse steps with her satchel pressed to her ribs. Mara's grey eyes moved over the flooded streets the way other people's hands move over prayer beads: counting, naming, refusing to lose anything. Mara was thirty-one, and she had been the city's only licensed cartographer for six of those years. Mara's hair was black, tied back with a length of tarred cord she had taken from a boat that no longer existed.

"You'll want to come up," said a voice above her. "The view is worse from up here, but it's honest."

The Warden of the Saltmere light came down three steps to meet her. Ilsa Corr was forty, broad-shouldered, with a lantern-keeper's stoop and a lantern-keeper's patience. In the early light the Warden's eyes were a deep brown, steady as the lamp behind her.

* * *

They climbed together. At the top, the great lens turned its slow circle over water that had been Fishmarket Row, Candle Lane and the tannery yard.

"I need the old survey," Mara said. "The one from before the wall."

"The archive is underwater."

"Then I need someone who can swim."

That someone arrived at noon, wet to the waist and grinning as if the flood were a private joke. Tomas Hale ran letters for the harbour office, and he carried the dispatch satchel on his right shoulder so that his writing hand stayed free. Tomas was left-handed; he signed every receipt with a hooked, backward-leaning scrawl that the clerks complained about and never corrected. Tomas had hazel eyes and a courier's habit of watching doors. Tomas, born in Varrow, had never learned to fear deep water.

Tomas was twenty-six, and he had swum the lower town twice already that morning.

"The archive door is jammed," he said. "But the window over it isn't."

*Isn't yet*, Mara thought, and began to sketch.
